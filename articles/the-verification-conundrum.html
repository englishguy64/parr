The Verification Conundrum

27 November 2026

There has been a shift in commercial production to verification of code produced by Claude Code and similar tools. Over the years, testing and QA have taken a back seat to rapid application development and deployment. Some of this was totally justified where requirements were fuzzy and final outcomes unknown. We have also seen software released with technical debt because of commercial pressures or resource constraints.

We seem to be turning the clock back to a more untrusting environment. Just as we could not trust developers to test their own code (although some zealots actually believed they could), we no longer trust AI agents to test theirs. This has led to the renaming of test/QA as verification.

Owing to the speed of AI agents, we now have more code to verify and, worse still, more verification to run that was often missed or deemed unnecessary to get the product out of the door. For example: secure programming, performance analysis, consistency of coding styles, best practices, code smells and load testing.

Even if you can accept that all of these can be performed, your verification is only as good as its criteria, i.e. acceptance tests based on real-world data. This brings us full circle back to the Waterfall problem and why early verification should be avoided. It has long been known in the digital world that front-end testing makes no sense in most user cases, as the ball is always moving.

While AI agents and frameworks such as Playwright can help with test automation, the number of possible user journeys that can accumulate is overwhelming, and that is without looking at the automation code.

The past tendency of the under-pressure development team, of course, was to skip some of this workload, but now that we have AI agents gating other AI-written code, that may not be so easy.

In reality, as the use of AI tools becomes normal and widespread across the industry, we will see old patterns associated with resource constraints and delivery deadlines reappearing. A new but not uncommon failing, the SPOF (Single Point of Failure), will start to appear more frequently where structures have been made too flat and high workloads given to too few people.

The added dimension of unrealistic expectation that more can be delivered for less, in less time, by AI may well lead to a return to higher recruitment or a scramble for the higher achievers (supply and demand).

What is clouding the picture today is recession and, to a degree, international political events leading to a suppression of confidence.

After all, we all like to buy when we are feeling safe about ourselves and our bank balance.
